Will this order arrive late? Supply chain risk with PySpark

Big data project in progress: predicting late deliveries on the DataCo smart supply chain dataset with Spark MLlib.
In progress · Big Data
Question

Can late deliveries be predicted from what is known when the order is placed?

Data

DataCo Smart Supply Chain: global orders with products, customers, markets and shipping

Engine

Apache Spark (PySpark DataFrames + MLlib pipelines)

Status

Pipeline design and data ingestion; results will be published here

NoteThis page is a living project log

The project is under way this semester. The design below is final; the results section will be filled in from the pipeline’s own output once the runs are complete, the same way every chart on the other pages is drawn from real result files.

Why it matters

A late delivery is rarely a surprise to the data: shipping mode, destination market, product category, order timing and the promised schedule all carry signal. If the risk is known when the order is placed, a company can choose a faster shipping mode, warn the customer, or reorder stock earlier. This is the business problem behind the project.

Why Spark

The dataset is large enough, and wide enough (dozens of columns of mixed types), that a single-machine pandas workflow becomes awkward to repeat and impossible to scale. The project uses Spark the way it would be used on a real cluster: lazy DataFrame transformations, a declared schema, and an MLlib Pipeline whose every step (indexing, encoding, assembling, scaling, modelling) is fitted on training data only and can be re-run on new data unchanged.

The pipeline

  1. Problem framing and datasetBinary target: late delivery risk. Business metric: recall on late orders, at a controlled false-alarm rate.
  2. IngestionSpark session, explicit schema (no type inference on a large CSV), basic profiling of every column.
  3. Cleaning and leakage auditColumns only known after shipping (real shipping days, delivery status, shipping date) describe the outcome and must be dropped, or the model learns the answer instead of the risk.
  4. Feature engineeringOrder weekday, month and hour; scheduled shipping days; shipping mode; market, region and country; product category; order value and discount.
  5. MLlib pipelineStringIndexer and OneHotEncoder for categories, VectorAssembler and StandardScaler, fitted on the training split only.
  6. Models and tuningLogistic regression (baseline), Random Forest and Gradient-Boosted Trees, tuned with CrossValidator on a stratified split.
  7. EvaluationROC-AUC, PR-AUC, F1 and recall on late orders; feature importance; error analysis by market and shipping mode.
  8. Scaling notesPartitioning, caching and run times, to show what changes when the data no longer fits on one machine.

The trap this project is designed to avoid

Target leakage. In this kind of dataset, some columns are recorded after the parcel has shipped. A model given “actual days for shipping” next to “scheduled days” can reach near-perfect accuracy by subtracting one from the other, and is useless in practice. The audit in step 3 decides, column by column, what is genuinely known at order time. Getting this right matters more than any choice of algorithm.
